Early this season I wrote that the tuskboars had come out of Ashridge, and that they were not the first sign that something had gone wrong in the forest. The first sign was the newts. Their pilgrimage to Lake Viralora had been thinning for years, and in the spring it did not come at all. I stood at the shallows under old Valvahr for three mornings, and nothing came.

I went back this week, because I had a feeling, and because a fae who has walked a forest his whole life is allowed one or two of those.

## The pilgrimage

They started before dawn. I heard them before I saw them, a soft wet rustle across the whole forest floor, as though the leaf litter had begun to breathe. Then the ground at my feet was moving with them: redbelly newts, thousands of them, small and dark-backed and burning orange underneath, all walking the same way.

They came over the deadfall and under the ferns and through gaps in the vines no wider than my thumb. They came down the bank of Ashwinder Creek and across it, where I have seen grown hikers turn back. They crossed the Vaelthir road at the Ashridge turning, where the overgrowth had shut it, and every Groves family who heard the news came out with a lamp and stood along the verge so no cart would roll through. Nobody had to be asked.

Most of them came in pairs. The old families say a newt that makes it home is never alone in the water. I used to think that was something you tell children. I stood at the shallows of Viralora at first light and watched them reach the water two by two, after a whole forest of trying, and I am not ashamed to say I sat down in the mud.

They come back to the water they began in. They find each other there, and they stay.

## The fire

Two nights before that, the Groves had their first thunder in longer than I have kept notes. It rolled in over the Greypeaks and sat over the forest for an hour. Just before dawn, lightning came down in the far Ashridge burn country, where the old fire scars are.

A small fire took. Rain followed it and put it out by noon, the way a forest fire is meant to go: a burn the size of a few farms, clean, with the cones cracking open in the heat and the ground cleared for whatever comes next. By evening the char was smoking in the drizzle. Maren Voss tells me the old foresters had a saying, that you could smell spring in the old fire. I could smell it from the ridge.

## The boars

For weeks I have reported on the Ashridge herd, which left the slope above Grove Hospital and sat in a line at the southern road where the Groves give way to the Faded Lands. They sat facing south like a congregation waiting on a sermon, and I could not tell you why.

The morning after the fire, they got up. I watched them come north up the edge of the road in single file, unhurried, past my camp and into the trees, straight for the burn country. In a season the fireweed will be up, and so will the bracken and the green flush that comes after a burn, and they will be eating where they belong. I suspect they knew before I did.

## The vines

The overgrowth has stopped. Households along Ashwinder Creek that were losing a room a night have lost nothing since the thunder, and the vines on the main stair at Grove Hospital are browning and letting go of the rail. The Council says the hospital will reopen once the wards are cleared. The DellRiven and Ashridge networks will reopen trail by trail. Watch your footing on the deadfall, and mind the new burn.

I have been walking this forest my whole life, and when it is well it has a spark to it, the same as a person does. In the spring I wrote that the spark had gone out. They say the old word for Valvahr means something close to keeper of the slain, that the mother tree was holding something and waiting for the right time to give it back. I stood under her this week with the water full of newts and the smoke coming off Ashridge, and I think she has given it back.

I will keep reporting on everything the old forest is trying to say. This week, for once, it has nothing to warn us about.

*Kael Dunwick is the Whispering Groves section's backcountry travel and gear correspondent. He would like to thank every family that stood on the Vaelthir road with a lamp. Sightings and conditions you have seen out there that did not sit right, or that finally did, can be sent by owl.*
