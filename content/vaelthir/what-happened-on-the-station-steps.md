My mind will not stop racing. I lay down for an hour when I got home and woke up shouting, with the officers cutting down the crowd on the steps all over again. I cannot get the smell of lamp oil out of my nose. My wife has been trying to wash the blood out of my coat since I came through the door. None of it is mine, and none of it is coming out. We are on the fifth wash. I had a notebook on the steps tonight. An officer took it from me at the bottom of them, so what follows is from memory, and where my memory is not sure, I have said so.

## It was a vigil turned massacre

The crowd outside the Office of Resource and Civil Management's Vaelthir station was larger tonight than on any evening before it, and there is a plain reason for that which the office will not give you. This was the week the Faded Lands Community Board printed the names of the twenty serpent-folk killed on the strip in the first week of the patrols. Mrs. Edna Thatch had asked the Archaeological Society to come to the steps tonight not to chant but to read those names aloud, and the families came up from the strip to hear them read.

They brought twenty lanterns, one for each of the dead, with the name painted on the glass in white. As Mrs. Thatch read each name, someone from that family climbed the steps, set the lantern down by the station doors, and went back down into the crowd. The rest of us held our own lanterns and kept quiet. I have stood outside that station for a fortnight of evenings, and it was the quietest crowd I have seen there.

The officers on the steps stood aside to let the families up. I want that on the record, because for the first hour of the evening the men on that line behaved like neighbours.

## The order

Mrs. Thatch had read fourteen names, the last of them Sable Orrin, who lit the lamps on the south road every evening, when a voice from the station doors called for the flames to be put out. It did not come from any officer on the steps. I looked, and I could not see who it was in the doorway.

Mrs. Thatch answered that there were six names left and asked for five more minutes, just enough time to finish reading the names. The request was denied, and the line was ordered down the steps before she had finished the fifteenth.

## The lantern

The office says a lit lantern was thrown and broke against an officer on the steps. That is true, but it does not say where it was thrown from.

When the line moved, the officers at the top began clearing the lanterns the families had set by the doors, and they did not put them out first. One of them, on the top step, picked them up two at a time and threw them out over the heads of his own line into the crowd. Three sailed over the officers and came down among us, still burning. The fourth fell short. It broke against the back of the neck of the young officer on the third step, and his collar went up.

That officer is Aldous Wren. His colleagues put him out with their coats, and so did a woman from the Society with her shawl, who was struck with a baton for it. I could read the name on that lantern as it left the man's hand, because it was a short name and the flame was behind it: PIP. Pip Coombes was seven. Her mother had climbed the steps to set it down within the hour.

The office tells you a crowd threw fire at its officers. The fire that burned Aldous Wren came from behind him, from the top of the steps, from his own colleagues.

## The backlash

Somebody on the line shouted that the crowd had thrown fire. It was not true, and I do not think the man who shouted it had seen what I saw. It did not matter. The line came down the steps with swords drawn.

I have covered this office for three years. I have never seen a Bureau officer draw on a crowd in Vaelthir, and I would not have believed it of the men I had watched step aside for the families an hour before. I am telling you what I saw. They cut down the people at the foot of the steps first, the families who had carried the lanterns up, because those were the people nearest. Then they kept going.

The crowd ran for the square, and the University gates on the far side of it were shut. They had been open at dusk. Somebody shut them while we were listening to the names. The people at the back could not get out, the people at the front could not stop, and I watched a crowd that had stood silent for an hour crush itself against a locked gate.

Then the serpent-mousers came.

I do not know how long it lasted. Long enough. It ended the way it had started, with a voice from the station doors. A whistle went three times, the handlers called the mousers back to heel, and the line walked back up the steps and stood where it had stood at dusk, swords still out, as if nothing had happened. A porter on the University side finally got the gates open, and the people who could run, ran. The rest of us stayed with the ones who could not.

On my way back across the square, an officer stopped me at the foot of the steps, took my notebook out of my hand, and told me to go home. I did not go home.

## The dead

When it was over, the neighbours who could still stand laid the dead out in a row under the University wall and covered them with the Society's banners, the ones that read Neighbours, Not Prey. I walked the row with a borrowed pencil. There were thirty. I could put a name to twenty-six. Pip Coombes's mother was the ninth in the row.

Before dawn the Bureau's wagons came onto the square and took them, banners and all. Families who asked where they were being taken were told to ask again at the station, and to bring a signed Right of Release form for each of their dead.

## The rest of the seven

The office lists seven officers hurt. I can speak to three of them.

The officer who "was struck from behind and fell the length of the steps" was not struck by anyone in the crowd. He was at the front of the line when the rear of it was ordered forward, and he went down under his own colleagues.

The officer "bitten on the forearm while making an arrest" was bitten. The Bureau brought two serpent-mousers onto the square tonight on leads, the first time I have seen them inside the city. When they were let loose, one went for a serpent-folk boy of about twelve just as the officer took hold of him to arrest him, and it bit them both. The officer is being treated for venom. The boy is in the row under the University wall.

I did not see the stone that struck the third officer. I will not pretend I did.

## The eleven

Eleven people were arrested at the front of the crowd. Mrs. Thatch was the first. She was still holding the list, and she asked the officer who had her by the arm whether she could finish reading it. He let her read one more. She chose Hollis Barrow, who was killed out past the second creek with a notebook in his hand, taking notes for a story. Then they took her inside.

The office calls Mrs. Thatch the instigator. She asked for five minutes.

## What the office does not count

The office will say that a small number of persons in the crowd reported minor complaints. Thirty people are dead. When the wagons had gone, I walked to the waystation infirmary and stood at its door until the Fifth Hour, and I counted twenty more who I do not expect all to live the week, and fifty more hurt as badly as any of the seven officers the office will ask you to pray for. I am filing the full account separately, because the people in it deserve a page of their own and not a line at the bottom of mine.

I took the badge numbers of the officers I saw strike people tonight. They were in the notebook the Bureau took from me at the foot of the steps. I am trying to get it back.
