I go to the student gala every year expecting to be charmed and instead I am usually humbled, and this year was no exception. The Caldwell Wing's annual exhibition is a chaotic, overhung, badly lit affair, and every canvas on the wall has more life in it than half of what hangs, permanently and expensively, in the rooms downstairs.

The reason, I think, is that the students still paint like people to whom time is happening. Their spring blossoms are not the untroubled, unfalling blossoms of the great names; they droop, they brown at the edges, they look as though the painter half expected them to fall. There is a small study near the east door, a girl's hands cupped around a candle, that I stood in front of far longer than is dignified for a man of my standing. Whoever painted it understands light better than most people twice her age, if there were people twice her age, which lately there do not seem to be.

Not all of it works, of course. There is a large and very serious canvas about Duty that I will not describe except to say it is brown, and there is a sculpture of a kraken assembled entirely from kitchen spoons that made me laugh out loud in a way the serious canvas very much did not. Go and see them both, and go soon. I will say one odd thing and leave it there: a piece I had marked to write about, a long grey landscape with a road running off the bottom of the frame, was gone from its hook by the second evening, and no one at the door could tell me where it went.

*Ron Rudson is the Chronicle's Arts Critic. He will be at the Nascium bar afterward, as ever, and welcomes disagreement by owl.*
