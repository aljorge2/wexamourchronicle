For three weeks the families of the thirty killed on the square were told to apply at the station. The Chair wrote to this paper's editor that the dead should be buried and done with. He did not say where they had been taken.

On Day 239 the families went and found out.

A clerk who walked out of the Vaelthir station last month remembered a line in the yard book: three wagons, out before dawn on Day 220, marked *sent south*. The south road has been closed past the second creek since the curfew, so the families went around the crossing by the old unmapped track Hollis Barrow once wrote about. There were forty of us.

A little over a mile past the crossing, the three wagons sat in a hollow below the road, the horses gone. The dead were still in them, under the Society's banners. I will not say more about it than that. The families have earned the right to decide what is written down.

We pulled them home by hand through the night. When we came back past the crossing at first light, the two officers posted there watched us go by, and one of them took off his cap.

Before we left the hollow I looked south once. The road climbs onto high ground, and at the top, where there is nothing on any map, a light was burning. Nobody would tell me what it was.

They were buried on Day 240, each in their own family's ground, under the banner they came home in. Lanterns were lit on every road that night. Nobody was arrested.

I had twenty-six of the thirty names. The families gave me the last four on the road:

- Garret Pike, 42
- Mabry Thresher, 30
- Isaura Penhallow, 20
- Briony Teal, 24

That is thirty.
