I want to be gracious about this, and I will try, though the piece I am answering did not extend me the same courtesy, so understand that whatever grace survives to the end of this page arrived here against real resistance.

Two weeks ago a man named Joey Vaelwick sat across from me for what he now describes, in print, as "a little under three hours," as though the number were an accusation. He then went home and wrote four pages about the human being he says he could not find. I have read them. I have read them more than once, which is more attention than the writing earns, and I have decided that the kind thing, the generous thing, is to assume he simply did not understand what he was looking at. So let me help him. Let me do the thing his whole segment claims to do and cannot, which is tell you what was actually in the room.

He makes a great deal of my rider. He counts the pages for you, four, as if paper were proof of vanity. Every serious person in the pictures travels with terms. The terms exist because a story is a machine and a machine has to be built to a specification or it throws its parts across the room and injures the audience. He quotes three of mine as though they were confessions. I will give them back to you as what they are, which is a philosophy of the work, and you may decide for yourselves whether a man should apologize for having one.

He says I require my character to win every fight. I do. I will tell you why, and it is not the reason he needed it to be. People come in from a hard season and a harder year and they sit in a dark room and for two hours they would like to watch someone who does not lose. That is not weakness. That is the entire purpose of the thing. A hero who loses is a mirror, and people did not pay to look in a mirror, they have one at home and it has been unkind to them lately. I give them the other thing. I have always given them the other thing. Mr. Vaelwick, who has never once stood in front of a full house and felt it decide whether to believe him, would not know how heavy that other thing is to carry.

He says I require the romance to end in my favor, that I cannot, by contract, be the one who is left. He reports this in a tone of great discovery, as though he had found a body under my floor. Yes. It is in the document. I have never understood why a man should sign up to spend three months pretending to be someone, and pay for the privilege with his own face, only to have that someone humiliated in the last reel for a screenwriter's idea of poignancy. Losing is not depth. Any fool can write a man being left. It takes no craft at all. What takes craft is the other thing, the harder thing, the thing I have built a career on, which is a man who stays standing. He calls that fear. I call it the only discipline in the business worth having, and I notice he was very moved by it, so moved he built his whole four pages around it, so perhaps it is not as small as he pretends.

Now to the part he wanted most, and circled the longest, and clearly went home happiest about. He says the picture failed. He says I sat across from him "in the same week the whole region quietly declined to show up." He says it with the little catch of a man who has waited his whole career to say a sentence like that to a face like mine.

Here is the truth, and he could have had it if he had asked instead of arranging. A picture opens the way a tide comes in, in stages, and the people who count houses in the first week are the same people who called the atomic clock a fluke and the Silence a wet spring. The audience for what I make does not arrive on a schedule that flatters a critic's deadline. It arrives. It always arrives. It arrived for me before Joey Vaelwick had a byline and it will be arriving for me after his is forgotten, and if the halls were thin the week he needed them thin, I would ask you to consider the possibility that a thing can be true for a week and false for a year. I have been in this a long time. I have watched rooms fill up late. I am not troubled. I want to be very clear, because he will read this too, and he will look for the tremor in it, and there is no tremor in it. I am not troubled.

What troubles me, if anything troubles me, is the ease of the cruelty. He took a man who gave him hours out of a working life and he made a specimen of him for the amusement of a readership that will forget my name by autumn and remember only that a clever writer once got the better of an actor who thought too well of himself. That is the story he wanted. It was easier than the one he was handed. I gave him a philosophy and he printed a diagnosis.

So I will end this the way I end everything, which he will find unbearable and which is precisely why I am doing it. I am closing the door on this newspaper. Not in anger, whatever he prints next. I simply decline to continue a relationship with a publication that has shown me what it does with the hours it is given. I am ending it. Me. Today. On this page, in my own words, in front of everyone, so that there can be no later version in which the Chronicle decided it was finished with Cassian Swiggity. It was the other way. It is always the other way. You may check the record. I have just written it.

*Cassian Swiggity is an actor. His new picture is playing now. He suggests you go and see for yourself what a room does when it is given the chance, and he thanks the very few of you who already have.*
