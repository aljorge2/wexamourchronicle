## Hollis Barrow, 46

Hollis Barrow wrote for this Board, and he wrote what the rest of us would not. When the strip started seeing people who were not there, Hollis gathered the reports because somebody should. When the whole of the outer reaches agreed never to speak of the south, Hollis went to the maps, the surveys and the grandfathers, weighed every piece, and put his name under one plain sentence: *There is something down there.*

He was patient with hearsay and impatient with silence. He never once called a thing proof when it was not. He knew every family on the strip and every road out of it, including the one that is on no map.

He was killed at dusk out past the second creek by the Bureau's animals, with his notebook in his hand. He was taking notes for a story about the creatures sitting on our southern border. Edna Thatch read his name last on the station steps, because they let her read one more and she chose him.

We sent this to the Chronicle when he died, and the Chronicle declined to print it. We are printing it now, and we are going to finish his story for him.

## The road south

The office reopened the south road past the second creek at noon on the day of the arrest. Eleven of us went that afternoon: the three of us left on the Board, and anyone else who wanted to come.

The crossing was empty. The children have taken that path every evening since the turn, and they say the woman with the silver hair has not been there at all. At the end of the second-creek lane the hollow house was dark and its door, shut since the fires, was standing open. Nobody was on the Downs road at dusk but us.

Past the creek the old road is just as Hollis described it: straight under the heather and the thornscrub, laid by people who meant to go somewhere and come back. It climbs. A little over a mile on we passed the hollow where the families found the wagons. A while after that the land came up onto high ground, flat and wide, and there was a fence.

## The station

The Plateau Research Station is a few low buildings, a gate, and a row of tall lamps facing south. Nobody was there. The doors were open and the wardens' cups were still on the table. One of the floodlamps had a broken lens. None of them was lit.

That was the light Merritt Vance saw from the hollow, the one nobody would tell him about. It has gone out.

## The stones

Beyond the station is a ring of standing stones, grey and taller than a barn, with the office's own white letters painted on the nearest three: T-1, T-2, T-3. The second and third are pitted low down with marks we did not want to look at for long.

Our grandfathers told us there was a country to the south with towers of stone and "hundreds of Ashren trees as far as the eye could see." From a distance, in poor light, that is what they look like: tall pale trunks with nothing at the top. Hollis filed it as hearsay. It was not hearsay. It was the stones.

Several of them have split open down the middle, cleanly, the way a seed splits. Water runs out of the cracks and down into the grass, and it is cold.

In the dust around the ring there were two sets of footprints, one larger than the other, side by side and keeping the same pace. They went all the way around the stones and stopped where they had started. They were fresh. There was nobody there, and there were no other prints coming or going. We walked round them, not over them. We did not know what else to do.

## Coming home

We started back at dawn. The phoenixes came up off the plateau behind us, the whole flock the University square lost, and went over our heads burning brighter than any of us had ever seen them. They lit the road all the way to the creek. Kelpies were coming up the creek beside us heading north, and their manes were going from white to black as they went. We heard later that the boars at the edge of the Groves had got up that same morning and gone home.

At the second creek three women passed us on the road, walking north together. We knew them. The woman in the blue gown from the hollow house was there, and the woman in green from the Downs road, and the tall one with silver hair from the crossing. The band of ice at her brow was dripping. None of them looked through us this time. The silver-haired one smiled at the children, and it was a real smile, not one held up by hand. She raised a hand to us as she went by.

They walked on up the road toward the strip, and we have not seen them since.

## The strip

The Byres' one green strip ran across the whole of their land overnight, and across the Attlebury plot, and the low farm past the old mile-marker. T. Ashe says his milk has kept three days. The strip is replanting, and anyone with seed to spare can write to the Board. The serpent-folk families who left quietly when the patrols came are coming home up the coast road, and their neighbours have the cottages aired.

Someone lit every lamp on the south road last night, all the way to the creek. Sable Orrin only got to the fourth. Nobody will say who lit the rest.

The Board is drawing the south onto the map. We will send a copy to the University.

There was something down there. Hollis said so and put his name under it. We are putting ours under his.
