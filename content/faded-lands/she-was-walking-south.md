I am not a person who writes to the paper. I want that understood before anything else, because half of you know me and the other half will by the time this prints, and I would not have you think I went looking to be in the pages. The Board asked, at the end of that piece a fortnight back, for anyone who had seen a thing to send it in. I have carried mine long enough. So here it is, and I will tell it plain, the way my mother told me to tell everything and then spent her whole life not telling the one thing that mattered.

I saw her on the low road past our fence, the evening before last, at that hour when the light goes the color of a bruise and you cannot yet say you need a lantern. A woman, grey shawl, walking. I took her at first for Sela's girl coming back late from the well, and I lifted my hand and called out did she want walking home, because that is what you do, that is the whole of how I was raised, you do not let a woman walk the strip alone at dusk. She did not turn. She did not slow. She kept on at that same even pace, and I stood at my own gate with my hand still up like a fool greeting the empty road, and the cold that went through me then was not the evening. I have felt cold evenings. This came up out of the ground.

Here is the part I have turned over every night since, and the part I almost did not send, because it is the part that stops being a ghost story and starts being something worse. She was going the wrong way. Our low road meets the Downs road, and the Downs road runs north, to the gate and the waystations and the lit world beyond, the way everything out here runs, the way it has always run. She was not walking north. She was walking away from all of it, past my fence, on toward the second creek and the place where the ruts give out and the map, they say, goes to pale paper. She was walking south. I watched her until the dark took her, and she never once turned her face toward the lights, and I have not been able to stop asking why a soul with nowhere left to be would choose the one direction none of the living will.

I told no one. That is my confession in this, and I think it is the confession of the whole strip. I was raised Evernal enough to believe that you keep your eyes on your own door and let what passes pass in silence, and I told myself that naming it would only feed it, and I said nothing for two days. Then at supper my father, who is not a fanciful man, who has buried more people than he has ever wept in front of, went quiet over his plate and said he had seen a light in the Hallorans' old place three nights running. The Hallorans have been gone since the fires. He said the light was not staying put. It had been in the north window the first night and the middle window the next and last night it was in the low south room, as if someone were walking through the dead house from one end to the other, slow, and would be out the far side and gone before the week was done.

And then my sister set down her cup and said she had not wanted to worry me, but the children would not take the crossing anymore, because of the pale woman who stands there at the blue hour watching the road, and my sister had gone to see for herself and had seen her, and had stood a while trying to work out what the woman was waiting for, and had come away certain of only one thing, which was that the woman was not watching the road north. She was facing the other way. She was looking down it, south, the way you look at a thing you have decided to do and have not yet found the nerve for.

Three of us at one table. Three different nights, three different figures, and not one of us had said a word until the silence broke all at once. And when we laid them side by side, the light moving south through the dead house, my woman on the low road going south past the fence, the pale one at the crossing facing south and gathering herself, the thing that emptied the warmth out of that kitchen was not that we had each seen a ghost. It was that we had each seen a ghost going the same way. They are not wandering. Wandering has no direction. These have a direction, and it is the one we were all of us raised never to ask about.

You printed a piece, not long ago, asking what lies south of the town, and you got four answers and no agreement, and the oldest folk told you the wise thing was not to ask at all. I read it and I nodded along like everyone. I am not nodding now. The dead are not asking. They have stopped asking and started walking, and they are all walking the one way, and I do not think a person gets to keep saying the south is nothing and empty and no reason to go when the dead of this strip are choosing it over the road to the gate and the lamps and everyone they left behind.

I will tell you the last of it and then I will stop, because it is the part I have not said aloud even to my father. I am not sure the woman on my road was a stranger. There was something in the set of her shoulders under that shawl. I have not let myself finish the thought, and I am not going to finish it here, in front of everyone, because I could be wrong and because I could be right and I do not know which one I could bear less. But I will tell you what frightened me worst, standing at my gate with my hand in the air. It was not that she would not turn to me. It was that for one moment, watching her go, I wanted to open the gate and go after her. I wanted it the way you want to sit down when you are tired. And I did not, and I am writing to you instead, with the gate shut and the lantern lit, so that I will have said it to someone.

So this is my account, since you asked for accounts. If you have seen your own, and you have been keeping the silence the way I kept mine, I am telling you that you are not the only one, and that it will feel kinder to say it than you fear. And I am telling you the other thing too, the only warning I have that is worth the ink. Do not offer her a ride. Do not follow the light through the empty house. And whatever you feel rise up in you when you watch one of them go, however much it feels like rest, do not go south after them. Shut your gate. Light your lamp. Write to the paper if you have to. But stay on the road that still has people on it.

*Nettie Combe farms with her family on the outer strip past the low road. This account was sent to the Faded Lands pages in answer to the Community Board's request. Anyone who has seen the like is asked to write to the desk by owl.*
