When I took up the stewardship of this paper a week past, I told you there would be changes in what we cover and in how, and I asked your patience while they took effect. This is the first of them, and I would rather say it plainly and under my own name than let you discover it one absent story at a time.

A newspaper is a household, and a household is steadiest when it keeps to what it knows. For some months now these pages have given a great deal of ink to things no one can quite stand behind. Lights that are said to leave a square in the night. A lake that empties itself. Women seen walking a southern road that no one else was on. Doors that are said to open only the one way. I do not stand here and call these accounts false. I say only that a responsible paper is not the place to nurse them, and that there is a wisdom older than the Chronicle in letting the small and the unproven pass into the quiet, rather than working it up into a headline that frightens good people before breakfast.

So we will be steadfast in what we are. We will print what can be verified, what serves the public order, and what helps a neighbour through an ordinary day, and we will let the rest rest. I am aware that some readers have grown fond of the other sort of story, and I ask them to trust that a calmer paper is a kinder one.

There is one matter on which we will not go quiet, because our readers have asked us not to, and they are right to ask. Our farmers on the strip and our walkers in the Groves have said, plainly and in numbers, that they wish this paper to take seriously the question of who is on their roads after dark, and what is being gathered from their fields and, in some tellings, from among their children. That is not alarm. That is a community minding its own gate, and this paper means to stand at the gate beside it.

I ask for your patience and your trust as all of this settles. The Chronicle keeps its own house now.

— Godfrey Ashmere, Editor
