I took the children back to Brindle this week to bury the frogs just as Posy Hartwell would have. She never walked past something dead without stopping to give it a proper send-off. So we brought a trowel and three small spades, and we dug a long shallow row above the waterline and laid them in it one by one.

When we arrived the tadpoles were gone. Seven days ago you could not see the mud for them, and now the bank was hopping with thousands of little frogs no bigger than a thumbnail. Every one of them had become a frog in less than a week, which any child in the Downs will tell you should take most of a season. Once the burying was done the children wanted to catch them, so here is how we did it:

1. **Wet your hands in the lake first.** A dry, warm hand hurts a frog's thin skin.
2. **Come from low and in front, never from above,** or you will look like a heron.
3. **Use a wide-mouthed jar** with a little lake water and a strand of weed, kept in the shade with the lid cracked.
4. **Always return them to the lake.** Before you go home, set every one back down at the water's edge where you found it.

<br>

We put them all back, or so I thought. My Posey hid a tiny frog in a jar, and I did not find it until we were halfway home. It was the size of a thumbnail when she caught it. By supper it filled the jar, fat and gold-eyed. In the morning it lay dead at the edge of our garden trough, its skin gone dull and loose, looking as old as any frog I have ever seen.

So now I think I know what happened to the frogs on the bank. They were old, not sick. Brindle is running whole lifetimes through in a matter of days, and I do not know why.
