I was not going to write this week. I have spent most of it on the Grange Hall steps with Luna, who has fed every family that came up from the strip for the burying, and I did not think I had a column left in me. Then I woke this morning to the sound of something I had not heard in my life. It was the blossom coming down.

It does not make much noise, falling blossom. But when every tree in the lane lets go on the same morning, you hear it, a soft dry hush like a page being turned very slowly. There were petals on every windowsill in the lane, petals in the hens' water and petals in Posey's hair. She stood in the yard with her hands out and her mouth open and asked me if the trees were broken.

I told her no. I told her they were finished, which is a different thing, and a good one.

## Brindle

I walked the children down to the footbridge before breakfast, because I could not keep from it, and because Posy Hartwell would have.

The kelpies are home. Their manes are black again, black as I remember them in the moonlight when I was a girl. The white is gone, and they look young enough to be foals. And there is not one of them. There are dozens, in the far reeds and the shallows and right up under the footbridge, nosing at the children's boots. Everyone in the Downs grew up believing Brindle had a single kelpie and that you were lucky to see it once in your life. Nana and Odile always said there were more, and that you see more of them the kinder you are to them. If that is true, then the Downs has been kinder this season than one column in these pages ever gave it credit for.

The tadpoles under the bridge are tadpoles, ordinary ones in ordinary numbers, and they will take most of the season to become frogs, the way they should. Posey checked. She will be checking every morning, I expect, for the rest of her life.

## The cake

The wheat in the middle fields has gone the faintest gold at the tips. The ice in our cellar has started to sweat. Aldworth stood at the woodpile last night for a long while and then said, "We will need more of this," which is the most he has said about a winter in his life. None of us has ever stacked wood against one. We will have to learn.

So I took down The Arbiter and made the cake. It was not the first true day of spring, because there has not been one of those in my lifetime. It was the first day the season moved, which Nana would have said was close enough. Three eggs, cracked by Posey, because it is her law now as well. I walked up the ridge for the honey. The Dorran looked out over his hives and said, "Bees busy," and would not take my coin.

I read the Dispatch while the cake was in the oven. I read every name in it. I read one of them twice, and I am not going to tell you which.

## The windowsill

When it had cooled I cut one slice and walked it round the lake to the stone house with the green shutters, which has stood empty since we buried Nana. I set it on the sill of the window that faces the hollow, where she set one every spring of her life. The door that never closed right was standing open, the way it always did.

This morning the slice was gone. There are no hens at the stone house now, and there was not a crumb on the sill. Something had been there in the night and taken it, and had been careful about it.

On the first morning of the Silence, Nana stood at that window and said, "Well. Somebody got their wish." I have carried those four words around my whole life. Whoever's wish it was, it has been given back.

## One more thing

Maud Pellingham had eleven pots in her kitchen that were not hers, and she was working on it. Maud was one of the nine at the University gates. Luna and I have been returning them this week, label by label, the way Maud would have wanted. If one of them is yours, it is at the Grange Hall, and it is clean.

Make the cake. Leave a slice. You know why now.
